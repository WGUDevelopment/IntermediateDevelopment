Make Intentional Practice Meaningful

Question Stacking vs. Question Scaffolding

Being Intentional About How We Ask Questions

Asking powerful questions is an important part of making intentional practice meaningful. However, the way we deliver those questions can be just as important as the questions themselves. Our goal is to create opportunities for students to think, explore, and make discoveries. Asking too many questions at once can unintentionally interfere with that process.

Avoid

Question Stacking

Asking multiple questions before giving the student an opportunity to think and respond.

Practice

Question Scaffolding

Asking one question at a time and gradually making the question more accessible when additional support is needed.

Question Stacking

Question stacking occurs when we ask multiple questions without allowing the student sufficient time to process and respond to each one. Even when each individual question is a powerful question, asking them all at once can diminish their effectiveness.

Peer Navigator
"What patterns do you see? How do those concepts relate to each other? How could you organize them differently?"
Question 1 Question 2 Question 3
What happens here?

Multiple questions compete for the student's attention. The student may only respond to one or may become overwhelmed.

Why We Need to Avoid Question Stacking

1

It Reduces Meaningful Engagement

When students are presented with multiple questions at once, they may focus on providing a quick response rather than taking the time to thoughtfully explore each question.

2

Questions Go Unanswered

If you ask three questions at once, the student will likely only respond to one. By asking those same questions separately and allowing time for each response, you create opportunities for the student to explore all three.

3

It Can Create Confusion

Students may be unsure which question they should answer first or may lose track of earlier questions while processing the most recent one.

4

It Limits Opportunities for Discovery

A student's response to one question may reveal an opportunity for a meaningful follow-up question that you would not have anticipated before hearing their response.

One Question at a Time

Ask a question, allow the student time to think, listen to their response, and use that response to inform your next question.

Question Scaffolding

Sometimes, even after providing adequate wait time, a student may struggle to respond to a powerful question. This does not necessarily mean that we need to abandon the question or provide an answer. Instead, we can scaffold the question by gradually narrowing its scope, making the thinking task more accessible while maintaining the original thinking objective.

Consider the following example of question scaffolding during intentional practice with a notetaking strategy. The Peer Navigator wants the student to recognize patterns and relationships between concepts.

Level 1 — Broad
Peer Navigator
"What patterns are you beginning to notice?"

Begin with an open-ended question that gives the student the freedom to explore and identify patterns independently.

20 seconds of wait time
The student is still struggling to respond.
↓
Level 2 — More Targeted
Peer Navigator
"Looking at the concepts you've written down, what similarities do you notice?"

Narrow the focus of the question by directing the student's attention to similarities between the concepts they have identified.

15 seconds of wait time
The student continues to need additional support.
↓
Level 3 — Highly Targeted
Peer Navigator
"Looking at these first two concepts, what is one thing they have in common?"

Make the thinking task more manageable by reducing the number of concepts the student needs to consider at one time.

5–10 seconds of wait time
↓

Scaffold Only When Needed

Use question scaffolding when it makes sense. If the student successfully responds to the initial broad question, there is no need to move to a more targeted question. The wait times in this example are recommended times, not fixed requirements. Give students the time they need to process and respond.

Maintaining the Original Thinking Objective

Notice that throughout the scaffolding example, the Peer Navigator's thinking objective never changes. The student is still being encouraged to recognize patterns and relationships between concepts. What changes is the scope of the thinking task. Each question makes the original objective more accessible without taking the thinking away from the student.

We Are Narrowing the Scope, Not Revealing the Answer

Question scaffolding is not the process of slowly revealing an answer or providing increasingly obvious hints until the student arrives at a predetermined response. Instead, it is about adjusting the level of support so the student can engage in the thinking process independently.

The Peer Navigator provides the structure. The student provides the thinking.

Before Scaffolding a Question, Consider:

  • Have I given the student sufficient time to think?
  • What type of thinking am I trying to encourage?
  • How can I make this thinking task more accessible without changing its purpose?
  • Does my next question still allow the student to make their own discovery?
