The Consequences of Teaching During Coaching

Understanding why maintaining the distinction between teaching and coaching matters for students, Course Instructors, and Peer Navigators.

Helping Students Without Taking Over Their Learning

As a Peer Navigator, you will encounter students who are confused, frustrated, or struggling to understand course material. Sometimes, the easiest response may seem to be explaining the concept, demonstrating how to solve the problem, or simply providing the answer.

These responses often come from a genuine desire to help. However, good intentions do not always lead to the best learning outcomes.

Peer Navigators serve an important role in supporting student success, but that role is different from the role of a Course Instructor (CI). Understanding the consequences of crossing that boundary helps us protect the student experience, strengthen our professional partnerships, and provide more effective coaching.

1

We Risk Providing Inaccurate Information

Peer Navigators are not expected to be subject matter experts (SMEs). Even when we have experience with a course or feel confident in our understanding of a particular concept, that does not necessarily mean we have the expertise needed to teach it accurately.

Course content may be more complex than it initially appears. There may be important exceptions, distinctions, or expectations that we are unaware of. When we attempt to explain academic content without the appropriate expertise, we risk giving students information that is incomplete, oversimplified, or incorrect.

Students may then rely on that explanation as they study, complete assignments, or prepare for assessments.

Consider This

A student asks a Peer Navigator to explain a concept that will appear on an upcoming assessment. The Peer Navigator remembers learning something similar and offers an explanation. However, the explanation leaves out an important distinction. The student leaves feeling confident but has actually developed an incomplete understanding of the material.

Why this matters: Students deserve accurate academic guidance. When subject matter expertise is needed, the Course Instructor is the appropriate resource.
2

We Undermine the Role of the Course Instructor

Course Instructors bring specialized academic knowledge and instructional expertise to the student experience. Their role includes helping students understand course concepts, addressing content-specific questions, and providing guidance related to academic expectations.

When Peer Navigators begin teaching course content, the distinction between these roles becomes blurred.

Students may begin to see Peer Navigators as an alternative to their Course Instructors, rather than recognizing the unique support that each role provides.

Over time, this can diminish the perceived value of the Course Instructor's expertise and make it less likely that students will seek support from the appropriate resource.

Consider This

A student regularly attends peer coaching appointments and asks content-related questions. Because the Peer Navigator repeatedly explains concepts and provides answers, the student stops reaching out to their Course Instructor. Eventually, the student encounters a more complex academic challenge but lacks an established connection with the person best equipped to help.

Why this matters: Peer Navigators and Course Instructors provide complementary forms of support. Our goal is to reinforce the value of both roles, not replace one with the other.
3

We Can Damage Trust Between Course Instructors and Peer Navigators

Effective student support depends on collaboration and mutual trust between the professionals who serve our students.

Course Instructors need to feel confident that Peer Navigators understand and respect the boundaries of their role. Likewise, Peer Navigators benefit from positive relationships with Course Instructors and clear pathways for connecting students with academic support.

When Peer Navigators teach content, particularly when their explanations conflict with what a Course Instructor has communicated, that trust can begin to deteriorate.

Course Instructors may become less confident referring students to peer coaching or collaborating with Peer Navigators if they are concerned about inaccurate or inconsistent academic guidance.

Consider This

A student tells their Course Instructor, "My Peer Navigator explained it differently." The Course Instructor now has to clarify the concept while also addressing the conflicting guidance the student received. Repeated experiences like this can create frustration and weaken confidence in the peer coaching program.

Why this matters: Respecting role boundaries demonstrates professionalism, strengthens partnerships, and helps students experience consistent, coordinated support.
4

We Take Away Student Ownership and Empowerment

Perhaps the most important consequence of teaching during a coaching session is the impact it can have on the coaching relationship itself.

Coaching is about empowering students to become more confident, capable, and independent learners.

Our responsibility is not simply to help students arrive at an answer. It is to support them in developing the thinking processes, learning strategies, and problem-solving skills that allow them to make progress independently.

When we explain the content, solve the problem, or provide the answer, we may unintentionally remove the very opportunity the student needs to develop those skills.

The Peer Navigator becomes the one doing the thinking, while the student becomes a passive recipient of information. This shifts ownership away from the student and undermines the purpose of the coaching relationship.

When We Teach

The Peer Navigator explains a concept, demonstrates the solution, or provides an answer.

The Peer Navigator does the thinking for the student.

When We Coach

The Peer Navigator asks thoughtful questions, supports the student's exploration, and helps them identify strategies or resources.

The student remains responsible for the thinking.

Consider the difference between a student who receives an explanation and a student who works through a challenging question with supportive coaching.

The first student may leave with an answer. The second student has an opportunity to leave with something more valuable: an understanding of how they approached the challenge, the confidence that they can work through difficulties, and a strategy they can apply again.

Why this matters: Student success is not just about understanding today's course content. It is also about developing the confidence, ownership, and independence needed to navigate future challenges.

Maintaining Boundaries Does Not Mean Withholding Support

Recognizing these consequences does not mean that Peer Navigators should avoid content-related conversations or immediately shut down a student who asks a question about course material.

Instead, it means being intentional about how we engage in those conversations.

We can help students explore what they already understand, identify where they are getting stuck, locate appropriate resources, and apply effective learning strategies without taking on the role of a Course Instructor.

For Example

If a student says, "Can you explain this concept to me?" a Peer Navigator might respond:

"Let's start with what you already know about the concept. Which part makes sense to you, and where are you starting to feel stuck?"

This keeps the student engaged in the thinking process while helping the Peer Navigator identify an appropriate coaching strategy or resource.

When a student needs an explanation requiring subject matter expertise, we can support them in connecting with their Course Instructor. That connection is not a failure to help; it is an important part of effective coaching.

The Bigger Picture

The boundary between teaching and coaching exists for important reasons. It protects the accuracy of the information students receive, respects the expertise of Course Instructors, preserves professional trust, and helps students develop ownership of their learning.

As Peer Navigators, we bring value to the student experience not because we know every answer, but because we know how to help students develop the skills to seek, evaluate, and work toward answers themselves.

Our goal is not to be the person who has the answers.
Our goal is to help students become confident in their ability to find them.
