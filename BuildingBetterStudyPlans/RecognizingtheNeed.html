Intermediate Development | Building Better Study Plans

Recognizing the Need

Understanding when students may benefit from developing a structured study plan and how to introduce planning through intentional coaching conversations.

What Is a Study Plan?

A study plan is a personalized roadmap that helps students organize their course material, establish learning goals, and identify the time, strategies, and resources they will use to make progress. Rather than approaching a course one topic at a time without a clear direction, a study plan helps students see the bigger picture and make intentional decisions about their learning.

A strong study plan answers: What do I need to learn? When will I work on it? How will I approach it? What support might I need along the way?

Study plans can help students feel less overwhelmed, establish realistic expectations, prioritize their learning, and recognize progress toward their course goals.

The student should maintain ownership of the plan. A Peer Navigator's role is to help the student explore possibilities, organize their thinking, and make informed decisions, rather than creating a schedule for them.

When Might a Student Need a Study Plan?

Sometimes students schedule a coaching appointment without knowing exactly what type of support would be most helpful. Before jumping into a random learning strategy, it may be more valuable to explore whether the student needs a structured plan for approaching the course as a whole.

1. Starting a New Course

"I just opened this course, and I'm not really sure where to start."

Students beginning a new course may benefit from exploring its structure, identifying major topics, reviewing available resources, and deciding how to organize their study time.

Planning opportunity: Build a roadmap before diving into individual learning strategies.

2. Preparing for a Pre-Assessment or First OA Attempt

"I'm getting ready for my assessment, but I don't know how to organize everything I still need to study."

A study plan can help students organize review activities, consider areas where they feel less confident, and identify resources that may support their preparation.

Planning opportunity: Develop a structured approach to assessment preparation rather than studying topics without clear priorities.

3. Preparing for an Assessment Retake

"I didn't pass my assessment, and now I feel like I need to start all over."

Students preparing for a retake may feel discouraged or unsure how to approach their next attempt. A study plan can help them organize their preparation, identify areas requiring additional attention, and incorporate Course Instructor support and approved resources.

Planning opportunity: Help the student translate their next steps and academic guidance into an actionable preparation plan.

Remember: These situations are indicators that study planning may be useful, not automatic reasons to create a plan. Use coaching questions to explore what the student needs before deciding together how to use the appointment.

Explore Before Suggesting

The Peer Navigator should not assume that a study plan is needed based solely on where the student is in their course. Instead, begin by exploring the student's current experience, goals, and challenges. Powerful questions can help students recognize whether they would benefit from taking a broader look at their course before focusing on a specific learning strategy.

If the conversation reveals that a student would benefit from more structure, the Peer Navigator might introduce study planning as an option:

"It sounds like you're trying to figure out how to approach the course as a whole. One option we could explore is building a study plan together. How does that sound to you?"

This approach introduces a potential strategy while keeping the decision collaborative and student-centered.

Study Planning Can Take an Entire Session

When a student is new to a course or unsure how to organize their learning, creating a study plan can be a meaningful first coaching appointment.

There is no need to rush into demonstrating a study strategy during the first session. Helping a student build a clear direction for their learning is valuable coaching in itself. In fact, study planning can create opportunities for more intentional coaching in future sessions.

1

Initial Session: Build the Roadmap

Explore the student's goals, review course topics, identify available resources, and collaboratively create a realistic study plan.

2

Follow-Up Appointments: Practice Strategies

Schedule follow-up appointments to practice learning strategies that align with the topics the student is currently studying.

3

Ongoing Coaching: Reflect and Adjust

Use future appointments to recognize progress, explore challenges, strengthen accountability, and adjust the study plan when needed.

Think of it this way: The initial study plan creates the direction. Future appointments provide opportunities to practice the strategies, reflect on progress, and continue building independent learning skills.

What About Live Events?

In a live event, identifying whether individual students need a study plan is less central because the Peer Navigator has already developed the session plan before students arrive. However, powerful questions are still essential. Live event facilitators can encourage students to reflect on their existing knowledge, course goals, and learning priorities while introducing tools they can continue using independently.

Example: Course Planning Live Event

A course overview or getting started live event could introduce two complementary planning tools:

First Half

Explore Existing Knowledge with KWL

Introduce the KWL chart and guide students in reflecting on what they already know, what they want to learn, and which course topics may need more attention.

Note: The next page in this learning path will explore the KWL strategy in greater detail.

Second Half

Introduce the Study Plan Template

Walk students through the study plan template, demonstrate how to organize course topics into manageable goals, and encourage them to consider their available time and learning resources.

Students can begin their plans during the event and continue personalizing them afterward.

The goal is introduction, not completion. Students do not need to finish their entire KWL chart or study plan during the live event. The purpose is to introduce the tools, help students understand how to use them, and encourage independent application after the session.

Because students participating in a live event may have different experiences, goals, and schedules, the facilitator should avoid prescribing one study plan for everyone. Instead, invite students to reflect on how the tools might work for their individual circumstances and encourage them to make decisions that support their own learning.

Collaborative Planning Is Coaching

Helping students develop a clear direction for their learning can be just as valuable as practicing an individual study strategy. By recognizing when planning would be useful and exploring the student's needs first, Peer Navigators can create more intentional, personalized, and empowering coaching experiences.
